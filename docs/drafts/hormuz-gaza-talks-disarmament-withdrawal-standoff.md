# Hormuz and Gaza Talks Stall Over Disarmament Demands

Diplomatic efforts aimed at easing two of the Middle East's most consequential conflicts are running into a similar obstacle: neither side wants to make the first irreversible move.

In the Gulf, Iran has linked a full reopening of the Strait of Hormuz to broad political, military and economic concessions from the United States. In Gaza, Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu says Israeli forces will not withdraw until Hamas has genuinely surrendered its weapons. Hamas, meanwhile, has tied disarmament to an end to Israeli attacks and a withdrawal of Israeli troops.

The disputes are separate and involve different actors, histories and security calculations. Yet both have become arguments about sequencing. Each side wants evidence that the other will comply before giving up leverage of its own.

## Hormuz talks advance without resolving the main dispute

Iran and Oman have been working on a temporary arrangement intended to make commercial passage through the Strait of Hormuz safer. The waterway connects the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman and is one of the world's most important routes for oil and liquefied natural gas shipments.

According to reporting by The Associated Press, the emerging plan would establish temporary entry and exit routes, with ships entering closer to Iran and leaving closer to Oman. The arrangement could be announced jointly by Iran, Oman, the United States and the International Maritime Organization, although its terms remain subject to change until a final announcement is made.

Oman has described the discussions in positive terms and urged all parties to avoid actions that could undermine progress. A practical shipping arrangement would offer some relief to tanker operators, insurers and energy markets that have been watching the strait closely.

But a temporary navigation plan is not the same as a political settlement. Iranian officials have said that full access to the strait remains connected to larger demands. Those demands include an end to military threats and operations, the withdrawal of United States forces involved in action against Iran, compensation for damage, sanctions relief and the release of frozen Iranian assets.

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi has also drawn a distinction between messages exchanged through intermediaries and formal negotiations. That distinction matters because it shows that communication has resumed without confirming that Washington and Tehran have reached a comprehensive negotiating process.

## Washington sees movement but not a finished agreement

United States officials have presented the talks as active but incomplete. Vice President JD Vance said the process was in the middle stages, cautioning against treating progress as a final breakthrough. President Donald Trump has also indicated that contacts are continuing while avoiding a claim that the broader confrontation has been settled.

The immediate American objective is clear: keep energy shipments moving safely and avoid additional pressure on global fuel prices. Washington has argued that temporary routes should operate without arbitrary approvals, tolls or other restrictions.

Tehran sees the strait as part of a wider security dispute rather than a stand-alone shipping problem. That difference in scope explains why a technical agreement can move forward while the central political argument remains unresolved. Oman may be able to help establish a safer corridor, but it cannot by itself settle every demand between Iran and the United States.

For markets, the distinction is important. A temporary shipping mechanism may reduce near-term risk, but uncertainty will remain as long as access can again become leverage in the confrontation.

## Gaza framework faces its own sequencing deadlock

The Gaza ceasefire process is confronting a comparable dispute over disarmament and withdrawal. Netanyahu has rejected a United States-backed framework in its current form and said Israel will not pull its forces out of Gaza until Hamas is fully disarmed. He has insisted that the process must remove both heavy and light weapons rather than produce a symbolic handover.

The proposed framework seeks to connect several steps: Hamas would begin disarming, Israel would halt attacks and withdraw in phases, and an international stabilization force would work with a reconstituted Palestinian police service on security.

Hamas has said it remains committed to a roadmap discussed with mediators, but its representatives have argued that Israel must stop military operations and begin withdrawing before the group gives up its weapons. That leaves both parties demanding proof from the other before acting.

The disagreement is more than a procedural detail. Disarmament is central to Israel's claim that Gaza must no longer pose a military threat. Withdrawal and a durable end to attacks are central to Hamas's calculation that surrendering weapons will not leave it exposed while Israeli forces retain freedom of action.

## Domestic politics narrow the room for compromise

Netanyahu is also operating under political pressure at home as Israel approaches an election in October. Far-right members of his coalition oppose concessions that they believe would leave Hamas with influence or military capacity. At the same time, the Trump administration wants visible progress toward ending the war.

Those pressures make sequencing especially sensitive. Moving first could be presented by political opponents as surrendering leverage without receiving a guaranteed concession in return. The same logic shapes the Gulf negotiations, where both Washington and Tehran face strong incentives to describe the other side as responsible for the impasse.

## What happens next

The most immediate development to watch is whether Iran and Oman formally announce their temporary shipping arrangement and whether commercial operators consider its guarantees strong enough to use. A corridor that works in practice could build limited confidence even without resolving the larger confrontation.

In Gaza, mediators will need to turn general commitments into a verifiable sequence. That could require agreed timelines, outside monitoring and clear consequences if either side fails to complete a promised step. Without those safeguards, demands for the other party to move first are likely to persist.

The common thread is not that the Gulf and Gaza conflicts are the same. It is that diplomacy in both cases has reached the point where statements of intent are no longer enough. Progress now depends on a sequence that gives each side credible security while preventing either from using delay to preserve the status quo.

Until negotiators solve that problem, temporary arrangements may reduce immediate danger, but a durable settlement will remain out of reach.
