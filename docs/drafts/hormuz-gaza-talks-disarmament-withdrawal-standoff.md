# Hormuz Shipping Deal Holds on Paper as Attacks Resume; Gaza's 'Ceasefire' Toll Tops 1,000

Two of the Middle East's most dangerous standoffs are both stuck in the same place: a diplomatic framework exists on paper, but neither side trusts the other enough to let it hold in practice.

In the Gulf, Iran and Oman spent August negotiating a temporary shipping corridor through the Strait of Hormuz, and by late August they had one. Within days, a tanker was hit by projectiles and Iran was adding more ships to a blacklist. In Gaza, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has rejected the Trump administration's 15-point disarmament plan outright, while the territory's ceasefire, technically in effect since October 2025, has a death toll that passed 1,000 months ago and keeps climbing.

Both disputes now share the same structural problem: an agreement that looks real in a press release doesn't survive contact with the underlying distrust driving each conflict.

## A Hormuz deal that lasted about a week before the shooting resumed

Iran and Oman announced in late August that they had settled on a shipping map for the Strait of Hormuz: inbound tankers would travel a northern lane through Iranian waters, outbound traffic would use a southern lane coordinated with Oman, and a joint coordination center would track vessel movements. Iran's parliament approved the general outlines, and negotiators reportedly agreed there would be no transit fees during the interim period, a concession aimed at reassuring shippers and insurers who had been paying a war-risk premium on Gulf routes for months.

It did not hold for long. On September 1, a tanker transiting the strait was struck by three unidentified projectiles. Two days later, Iran added 11 more vessels to a blacklist of ships it deems non-compliant, subjecting them to fines, confiscation or detention if they attempt passage. US Vice President JD Vance drew a hard line in response: "We are not talking to the Iranians, and we are not going to talk to them unless they stop shooting at commercial shipping." Vance also claimed oil flow through the strait was nearing pre-war volumes, citing roughly 15 million barrels moving through the channel in a recent stretch, while blaming elevated US gasoline prices on the Iranian attacks.

## Why a signed arrangement didn't stop the attacks

The gap between the paper agreement and the shooting comes down to what each side actually settled. Iran's Foreign Minister, Abbas Araghchi, has been explicit that the shipping-lane arrangement with Oman is a narrow, technical fix, not a resolution of the underlying war. "It depends on fulfilling other conditions," he said of any fuller reopening of the strait, listing demands that include a formal end to hostilities, the withdrawal of US forces involved in the conflict, compensation for war damage, sanctions relief and the release of frozen Iranian assets. None of those conditions were part of the Oman deal.

That distinction matters because it means the corridor was never meant to substitute for a ceasefire, only to reduce collateral risk to civilian shipping while the larger war continued. President Trump captured the ambiguity of the moment when he told Axios the two sides were "only semi-negotiating." Iran's blacklist and the September 1 tanker strike are consistent with a country still fighting a war at sea while simultaneously trying to carve out a protected lane for the traffic it wants to keep moving.

For markets and shipping insurers, that combination, a working technical corridor alongside continued attacks outside it, may be the realistic near-term outcome rather than a transitional step toward full normalization.

## Gaza: a rejected plan and a ceasefire death toll that keeps rising

Netanyahu's government formally rejected the White House's 15-point Gaza plan in early August, and the core disagreement has not moved since. The plan called for a phased process: Hamas would begin surrendering its heaviest weapons, weapons-production sites and tunnel network first, while Israeli forces began a parallel, staged withdrawal. Netanyahu rejected that sequencing outright, saying Israeli forces "will not carry out any withdrawal until Hamas is genuinely disarmed," including "all weaponry," not a partial or symbolic handover.

Hamas has tied its own compliance to a different set of conditions: an end to Israeli strikes and a troop pullback to the "yellow line" established under the October 2025 ceasefire agreement. Bassem Naim, a member of Hamas's political bureau, said the group "expects mediators and the U.S. to pressure Netanyahu" rather than expecting Hamas to move first.

The deadlock would be easier to read as a paperwork dispute if the ceasefire itself were holding. It is not, at least not in the way the word usually implies. Gaza's health ministry reported 854 people killed in the territory since the October 2025 ceasefire took effect as of mid-May, a figure that had risen to 933 by early June and topped 1,000 within weeks after that, according to reporting at the time. Israeli strikes, shelling and gunfire along the boundary separating Israeli- and Palestinian-controlled zones have continued on a near-daily basis throughout the year the agreement has technically been in force. On September 1 alone, Israeli strikes killed at least five people in Gaza City, according to local reporting, underscoring that the disarmament standoff is unfolding against a backdrop of ongoing, not paused, violence.

## An election is now forcing the sequencing question

Israel's Knesset dissolved in mid-July, and Netanyahu's coalition has set the national election for October 27, the last legal date available and the first Israeli election since 1988 that was not called early. That timing puts real pressure on how Netanyahu handles both fronts. Far-right coalition partners have opposed any concession they see as leaving Hamas with residual military capacity, and a visible Gaza withdrawal ahead of a tight election would be a difficult sell to that flank regardless of what guarantees accompanied it.

The same dynamic runs through the Gulf negotiations in a quieter form: both Washington and Tehran have strong incentives to frame the other as the party blocking progress, since neither government wants to be seen conceding ground first.

## A separate but related sign of diplomatic motion

Not every track in the region is stalled. On September 2, US envoys, Netanyahu and Lebanese officials finalized an arrangement for Israel to release Lebanese detainees, with one prisoner, Malek Ghazi, transferred on September 3 and four more scheduled to follow the next day. A State Department official described the exchange in reciprocal terms: "Israel is releasing detainees and Lebanon is helping Israel recover the lost remains of Lebanese Jews." Further US-mediated talks between Israel and Lebanon are reportedly planned in the coming weeks.

It is a small, bounded success next to the scale of the Hormuz and Gaza standoffs, but it shows that US-brokered diplomacy in the region can still produce concrete, executed steps when the demands on each side are narrow enough to verify quickly.

## What to watch next

On Hormuz, the test is whether the Iran-Oman shipping lane keeps functioning for commercial traffic even as Iran continues to blacklist vessels and sporadic attacks continue outside the corridor. A corridor that survives alongside continued hostilities would be a strange kind of stability, but it may be the only kind on offer while Iran's broader list of conditions remains unmet.

In Gaza, the operative question is no longer really about the text of a disarmament plan. Israel and Hamas have both said, in different ways, what the other must do first. What has not materialized is a verification mechanism, monitors, timelines, or consequences for either side breaching its commitments, that would make either party confident enough to move. Absent that, the rising ceasefire death toll suggests the current arrangement is better described as a lower-intensity continuation of the war than a transition out of it.

Both conflicts have reached a point where announcements of frameworks and shipping maps are no longer, by themselves, evidence of progress. The next real signal will be whether the casualty and attack figures start falling, not whether another document gets signed.
